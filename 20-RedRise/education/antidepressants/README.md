# Understanding Antidepressants

This section explains what antidepressants are, how they work, why they cause withdrawal, and how to approach tapering safely.

---

## What Are SSRIs and SNRIs?

**SSRIs** (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors) and **SNRIs** (Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors) are the most commonly prescribed antidepressants.

| Type | Examples | Mechanism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| SSRIs | Prozac (fluoxetine), Zoloft (sertraline), Lexapro (escitalopram), Paxil (paroxetine) | Block the reuptake of serotonin, increasing its availability in the brain |
| SNRIs | Effexor (venlafaxine), Cymbalta (duloxetine), Pristiq (desvenlafaxine) | Block the reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine |

---

## Why Were They Developed?

- 1980s: The "chemical imbalance" theory became the dominant explanation for depression
- SSRIs were marketed as a safe, targeted solution to correct this imbalance
- They became blockbuster drugs, with sales reaching billions annually

---

## Common Indications

- Major depressive disorder
- Generalized anxiety disorder
- Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)
- Panic disorder
- Chronic pain (e.g., fibromyalgia, neuropathy)

---

## The Problem with Long-Term Use

- **Neuroadaptation** – the brain adapts to the presence of the drug, leading to physical dependence
- **Tolerance** – the drug becomes less effective over time
- **Withdrawal** – stopping the drug triggers a cascade of symptoms as the brain readjusts
- **Side Effects** – long-term use carries significant physical and emotional costs

---

## Why Withdrawal Happens

When you take an SSRI or SNRI, your brain adapts to the drug by altering neurotransmitter systems. When you stop, your brain needs time to rewire itself. This is not a sign of weakness or relapse—it's a predictable physiological response.
